Culturally inspired

A plant woven through every ceremony

The banana plant has been a culturally significant crop for many East African communities. It featured in birth, marriage and death ceremonies. It is a seasonless crop that saw people through times of hunger, and its smooth puree is still used to introduce infants to solid food.

It provided building materials, especially for thatching houses, and served as utensil and serving dish in ceremonial offerings and festivals. Its fruit has been used for centuries to make healthy traditional brews, both raw and ripened.

Many of those uses were led by the traditional woman , in food and brew preparation, in thatching, in weaving. That is who you see on every bottle.

Empowerment rooted

The glut that started the winery

Banana suckers were distributed across many parts of Kiambu during our founder's service as Women's Representative. The trees matured, fruit production began , and the resulting glut brought low prices and waste. It became apparent that fruit alone could not deliver the economic empowerment that had been envisaged.

Visiting homes and witnessing hunger, and lack of basics like sugar, yet bunches of bananas hung about in the compound , that made me embark on a journey of more meaningful value addition.

That journey set out to address two things at once: the banana-fruit glut and its post-harvest losses, and the use of the entire banana plant , not just the fruit.

In the bottle

Dry, and honest about it

Banny’s is a dry banana wine , fermented out rather than left sweet, so the fruit reads as fruit and not as syrup. Serve it well chilled at 8–10 °C. Once opened, keep it refrigerated.
